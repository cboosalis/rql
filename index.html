The question determines the approach

One concern supports different kinds of inquiry

       WHAT DO WE WANT TO KNOW?
                 │
      ┌──────────┼──────────┐
      │          │          │
 EXPERIENCES?  RELATIONSHIP?  BOTH?
      │          │          │
 Qualitative  Quantitative   Mixed
      │          │          │
  Accounts     Measures   Integration

Questions about experiences invite accounts and observations. Questions about relationships require defined measures. A mixed methods question requires a planned connection between numerical patterns and participants’ accounts.

Examples: How do students experience discussion? Is participation associated with comprehension growth? How do accounts help explain a measured pattern?

A quantitative relationship question

Participation and comprehension growth both need clear measures. An observed association would not establish that discussion caused growth; instruction, prior learning, and access may also matter.

Aligned question: Is participation in structured discussion of grade-level texts associated with reading-comprehension growth among multilingual Grade 6 learners?

Research questions and literature

Three quantitative question types

The wording changes the claim

      QUANTITATIVE QUESTIONS
                │
     ┌──────────┼──────────┐
     │          │          │
 RELATIONSHIP  DIFFERENCE   EFFECT
     │          │          │
 Do X and Y   Do groups   Does an
  covary?      differ?  intervention
                            work?

Relationship asks whether two measures vary together. Difference asks whether defined groups show different outcomes. Effect asks what an intervention changes relative to a credible alternative and requires a causal design.

Aligned questions: Is participation associated with growth? Do gains differ by access to discussion? Does assignment to structured discussion produce greater gains than existing instruction?

Numerical data can locate a pattern; student accounts can clarify how discussion is experienced. A mixed methods study plans how the findings will be connected, including when they disagree.

Aligned question: What reading-comprehension patterns appear, and how do students’ accounts of text discussion help explain those patterns?

What is known and what remains open

A literature gap must be demonstrated

   WHAT PUBLISHED STUDIES SHOW
               │
               ▼
   POPULATIONS • SETTINGS • METHODS
               │
               ▼
  WHAT THEY DO NOT YET ESTABLISH
               │
               ▼
     A JUSTIFIED STUDY QUESTION

A review can examine how prior studies define discussion, whose experiences they include, and how they measure comprehension. Only the reviewed evidence can justify a claimed knowledge gap. EDLP’s local equity gap is a separate claim supported by local outcome data.

Review question: What has research established about multilingual learners’ participation in discussion of grade-level texts?

A reported reading difference justifies inquiry when its year, group, measure, and denominator are clear. Records and educational-partner perspectives can test possible explanations, including access to grade-level texts and discussion opportunities.

EDLP inquiry: What school conditions shape the focal group’s access to grade-level text discussion, and what evidence supports or challenges each explanation?

For an EDLL study, the review helps define participation and choose fitting observations, interviews, or assessments. For a project, it can justify design principles. For EDLP, it suggests conditions to investigate locally and strategies worth examining.

Working test: Which prior finding changes how you define, observe, or interpret text discussion?
